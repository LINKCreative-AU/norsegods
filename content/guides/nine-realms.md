Norse cosmology imagines a set of worlds connected by the branches and roots of a single vast tree, and popular culture has settled on calling them "the Nine Realms" — Asgard, Vanaheim, Alfheim, Midgard, Jötunheim, Svartalfheim, Niflheim, Muspelheim, and Helheim. That number and that specific list are real enough to be useful, but it is worth knowing upfront: no medieval source ever lays out those nine names in one place as a finished chart. The number nine comes from scattered references to "nine worlds," while the actual roster is a modern reconstruction assembled from names that appear individually, and sometimes inconsistently, across the Poetic Edda and Prose Edda.

## Yggdrasil: The Tree That Holds the Worlds

Everything in this cosmology hangs on **[Yggdrasil](/concepts/yggdrasil)**, the immense ash tree whose branches and roots are said to reach through all the worlds. The Poetic Edda's *Grímnismál* and *Völuspá* both describe it in fragments: three roots, each reaching to a different place, with wells beneath them — [Urðarbrunnr](/concepts/urdarbrunnr) where the [Norns](/concepts/norns) tend the tree and shape fate, [Mímisbrunnr](/concepts/mimisbrunnr) where Odin traded an eye for wisdom, and [Hvergelmir](/concepts/hvergelmir), the roaring spring in the far north from which many of the world's rivers are said to flow. The dragon [Níðhöggr](/creatures/nidhogg) gnaws at Yggdrasil's roots from below, an eagle sits in its highest branches, and the squirrel [Ratatoskr](/creatures/ratatoskr) runs the trunk carrying insults between them. The tree is not a map so much as the mythology's central image of connection and cost — a structure so vast it holds every realm, and so besieged that its roots are being eaten even as it stands.

## Asgard: Home of the Æsir

**[Asgard](/realms/asgard)** is the stronghold of the Æsir gods, ruled by Odin and reached from Midgard by the burning rainbow bridge [Bifröst](/concepts/bifrost), which the god [Heimdall](/gods/heimdall) guards without sleep. Within its walls stand the gods' individual halls, chief among them Odin's [Valhalla](/concepts/valhalla), where the [einherjar](/concepts/einherjar) — warriors chosen from the battle-slain — feast and fight in preparation for Ragnarök. The wall of Asgard itself has its own myth: it was built by a giant mason who nearly won Freyja, the sun, and the moon as payment before the gods, with Loki's help, cheated him out of it, told in [the building of Asgard's wall](/events/building-of-asgards-wall).

## Vanaheim: Home of the Vanir

**[Vanaheim](/realms/vanaheim)** is the home of the Vanir gods — Njörð, Freyr, and Freyja's people before the [Æsir–Vanir War](/events/aesir-vanir-war) brought them to live among the Æsir. It is, honestly, the thinnest realm in the entire cosmology: the sources name it and confirm the Vanir live there, but no surviving myth is actually set within it. Nothing describes its landscape, its halls, or its geography relative to the other worlds — a gap worth stating plainly rather than filling in with invention.

## Midgard: The World of Humans

**[Midgard](/realms/midgard)** ("middle enclosure") is the human world, shaped by Odin and his brothers from the flesh of the slain giant [Ymir](/giants/ymir) and ringed by an ocean in which the World Serpent [Jörmungandr](/creatures/jormungandr) lies coiled, biting its own tail. The first man and woman, [Ask and Embla](/events/askr-and-embla), were carved from driftwood and given life by Odin and his brothers, and Midgard remains the stage for most of the mythology's encounters between gods, giants, and mortals — Thor's journey to test his strength against [Útgarða-Loki](/giants/utgarda-loki), or his day of fishing for the World Serpent, told in [Thor's fishing trip](/events/thors-fishing-trip).

## Jötunheim: Land of the Giants

**[Jötunheim](/realms/jotunheim)** is the realm of the giants, a wild and mountainous country generally imagined at the edges of the ordered world, though the sources are loose about its exact position relative to Midgard. It is home to figures like [Þrymr](/giants/thrym), who steals Mjölnir in one of the mythology's most comic tales, [the theft of Mjölnir](/events/theft-of-mjolnir), and [Útgarða-Loki](/giants/utgarda-loki), whose hall of illusions humbles Thor in [the journey to Útgard](/events/journey-to-utgard). Jötunheim functions less as a single described country than as shorthand for "wherever the giants are," which shifts depending on which myth is being told.

## Alfheim: Home of the Light Elves

**[Alfheim](/realms/alfheim)** is named in *Grímnismál* as the home of the light elves and, intriguingly, as a gift given to the god Freyr as a "tooth-gift" in infancy — meaning it is associated with Freyr's household even though he is Vanir, not an elf. Beyond that single stanza, the sources say almost nothing else about Alfheim or the elves who live there; the developed elf-lore familiar from later folklore and fantasy fiction is a much later accretion onto a genuinely sparse mythological foundation.

## Svartalfheim: Home of the Dwarves

**[Svartalfheim](/realms/svartalfheim)** ("black-elf home") is generally identified with the underground world of the dwarves, though the terminology is another place the sources blur together — some texts use "black elves" and "dwarves" (*dvergar*) as though interchangeable, others distinguish them, and the name Svartalfheim itself appears rarely, more often in Snorri than in the older poetry. It is the domain associated with master craftsmen like [Brokkr and Sindri](/creatures/brokkr-and-sindri), who forge Mjölnir and other treasures of the gods, and the [dvergar](/creatures/dvergar) generally, including figures like [Andvari](/creatures/andvari), whose cursed gold ring drives the tragedy of [the Otter's Ransom](/events/otters-ransom).

## Niflheim: World of Mist and Ice

**[Niflheim](/realms/niflheim)** is one of the two primordial realms present before the world existed, a land of fog, cold, and ice whose name means "mist-home." *Gylfaginning* places the roaring spring [Hvergelmir](/concepts/hvergelmir) here, source of many of the world's rivers, and describes Niflheim's ice meeting the sparks of fire from Muspelheim across the void of [Ginnungagap](/concepts/ginnungagap) to spark creation itself, told in [the creation of the world](/events/creation-of-the-world). Niflheim is sometimes conflated in popular retellings with Hel's realm of the dead, but in the primary sources it is distinct — a cosmic ice-realm from before time, not the afterlife itself.

## Muspelheim: World of Fire

**[Muspelheim](/realms/muspelheim)** is Niflheim's opposite number, a realm of pure heat and flame that existed before the ordered world, home to the fire-giant **[Surtr](/giants/surtr)**, who stands guard at its border with a flaming sword. Muspelheim's role in the mythology's opening act — its sparks meeting Niflheim's ice to quicken the void of Ginnungagap — is matched by its role in the closing one: at Ragnarök, Surtr leads Muspelheim's fire-giants against the gods and is said to engulf the world in flame at the very end.

## Helheim: Realm of the Dead

**[Helheim](/realms/helheim)**, often simply called Hel after its ruler, is where those who die of sickness or old age go — as opposed to the battle-slain, who may go to Valhalla or Fólkvangr instead. It lies far to the north and down, reached by a long, dark road that Odin's son [Hermóðr](/gods/hermodr) rides on Sleipnir to plead for Baldr's release, guarded at its gate by the giantess [Móðguðr](/creatures/modgud) and the hound [Garmr](/creatures/garm). Its ruler, the goddess **[Hel](/gods/hel)**, is Loki's daughter, appointed by Odin to govern this realm and, the sources make clear, not eager to give anyone back once they arrive.

## Where Does "Nine Realms" Actually Come From?

It is worth being direct about this: the neat nine-realm list is a modern synthesis, not a medieval one. The number nine itself is genuinely Eddic — *Völuspá* has the seeress recall that she remembers "nine worlds" (*nío heima*) and *Vafþrúðnismál* mentions Niflheim as one of "nine worlds" beneath its own kind of reckoning — so the count is not invented. But neither poem, nor Snorri's *Prose Edda*, ever lists nine names together in a single passage the way modern summaries do. What we actually have are individual realm-names scattered across different poems and prose sections — Asgard and Midgard constantly, Vanaheim, Álfheim, and Jötunheim by name in *Grímnismál*, Niflheim and Muspelheim in *Gylfaginning*'s creation account, Svartálfaheim mentioned occasionally in Snorri, and Hel's realm described without ever being formally counted among the rest. Modern popularizers, working backward from the poem's claim of "nine worlds," assembled the most commonly named realms into the nine-item list now found on posters and in video games. It is a reasonable and useful reconstruction — the realms it names are all real, attested places in the mythology — but readers should know it is an assembly, not a quotation.

## FAQ

**What are the Nine Realms in Norse mythology?**
The commonly cited list is Asgard, Vanaheim, Alfheim, Midgard, Jötunheim, Svartalfheim, Niflheim, Muspelheim, and Helheim, all connected by the world tree Yggdrasil. This exact nine-item list is a modern scholarly and popular reconstruction; the medieval sources mention "nine worlds" as a number but never enumerate all nine by name in one place.

**Is the Nine Realms list accurate to the original myths?**
Every realm on the list is genuinely attested in the Poetic Edda or Prose Edda, so the list is not invented — but its tidy, single-source presentation is. The realm names come from different poems written at different times, and some, like Vanaheim, are barely described at all beyond being named.

**Where do humans live in Norse cosmology?**
Humans live in Midgard, the "middle enclosure," ringed by an ocean where the World Serpent Jörmungandr lies coiled and connected to Asgard by the rainbow bridge Bifröst.

**What realm do you go to when you die in Norse mythology?**
It depends on how you died. Warriors chosen from those slain in battle may go to Odin's Valhalla or to Freyja's hall Fólkvangr; most others who die of illness or old age go to Helheim, ruled by the goddess Hel.

**Is Niflheim the same as Hel?**
No, and this is a common confusion. Niflheim is a primordial realm of mist and ice that existed before the ordered world was created, mentioned in the creation myth alongside Muspelheim. Hel's realm of the dead is a separate place, ruled by the goddess Hel, though later popular usage sometimes blurs the two together.
