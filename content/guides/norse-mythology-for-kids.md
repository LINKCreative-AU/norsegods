Norse mythology is the collection of old stories that people in Scandinavia told more than a thousand years ago, about gods who could be brave and wise and also stubborn and silly, giants who were sometimes enemies and sometimes family, and a giant tree that holds the whole world together — and it's a wonderful place for kids to start exploring myths because the stories are full of adventure, jokes, and characters who feel surprisingly real. This guide introduces the most important gods and ideas in simple language, and points you toward the gentlest stories to read first.

## Who are the main gods?

**[Odin](/gods/odin)** is the oldest and wisest of the gods, often pictured as a tall man with one eye, a wide-brimmed hat, and a long grey beard. He gave up his other eye just to gain more wisdom, and he's always seeking knowledge — from ravens, from wise giants, even from the dead. Kids sometimes find him a little mysterious, and that's fair: even in the old stories, nobody is quite sure what Odin is planning.

**[Thor](/gods/thor)** is probably the easiest god to love right away. He's huge, red-bearded, incredibly strong, and carries a hammer called Mjölnir that can crush mountains and always flies back to his hand when he throws it. Thor isn't the cleverest god, but he's kind-hearted underneath the bluster, and he spends most of his stories protecting people — gods and humans alike — from giants and monsters.

**[Loki](/gods/loki)** is trickier to explain, because he isn't simply good or simply bad — he's a shape-shifter and a trickster who causes trouble, tells jokes, solves problems he sometimes caused himself, and occasionally does something genuinely cruel. Younger kids usually enjoy him as mythology's mischief-maker; older kids can start noticing that his story gets darker the further into the myths you go.

**[Freyja](/gods/freyja)** is a beautiful and powerful goddess connected to love, but also to magic and even battle — she rides in a chariot pulled by cats and owns a falcon cloak that lets her fly. She's one of the strongest gods in all the myths, and stories about her often show her as clever and independent rather than helpless.

Other gods worth meeting: **[Frigg](/gods/frigg)**, Odin's wife, who is wise about the future; **[Týr](/gods/tyr)**, brave and honest, famous for a huge sacrifice involving a wolf; **[Heimdall](/gods/heimdall)**, the ever-watchful guardian who can hear grass grow; and **[Baldr](/gods/baldr)**, gentle and much-loved, whose story is one of the saddest in the whole mythology (more on that below).

## Where do the gods live? The nine realms and the great tree

Norse mythology imagines the whole universe held together by an enormous ash tree called **[Yggdrasil](/concepts/yggdrasil)**, so big that its branches reach over the sky and its roots dig down into other worlds. Around and along this tree are the **[nine realms](/guides/nine-realms)** — different homelands for different kinds of beings. Gods live in Asgard, humans live in Midgard, giants live in Jotunheim, and there are realms for elves, dwarves, and even the dead. You don't need to memorize all nine to enjoy the stories, but it helps to know that the Norse universe isn't just "heaven and earth" — it's a whole neighborhood of worlds, all connected by one great tree.

## Why do the gods have such strange names?

Old Norse, the language these stories were first told in, sounds different from English, so names like Yggdrasil, Mjölnir, and Ragnarök can look intimidating on the page. A good trick for reading with kids: don't worry about perfect pronunciation, just pick a way to say each name and stick with it consistently — the stories work fine even with an approximate accent, and a lot of the fun is in kids inventing their own way of saying "Yggdrasil" with confidence.

## Which myths make the best first stories?

Some Norse myths are perfect for young readers: funny, adventurous, and low on scary content. Good starting points include Thor accidentally dressing up as a bride to get his hammer back, Thor and Loki being tricked by clever giants in a hall of illusions, and the story of how the gods got their finest treasures — including Thor's hammer — from a bet with mischievous dwarves. These stories have jokes, disguises, and happy endings, which makes them ideal for reading aloud. The site's [bedtime stories](/stories) collection is written specifically for this kind of gentle, read-aloud retelling, and it's the best place to start if you want myths already shaped for a young audience.

## A note for parents: gentle myths vs. darker myths

Not every Norse myth is right for every age, and that's completely normal — this mythology was told to adults as often as children, and some of it deals with real loss and darkness. As a rough guide: stories about Thor's adventures, contests with giants, and clever tricks tend to be the gentlest, full of humor and short on real danger. Stories about **[Loki](/gods/loki)** get more complicated the further you go, since his mischief eventually causes real harm. The story of **[Baldr](/gods/baldr)**'s death is genuinely sad — it's a myth about grief, and it's worth previewing before sharing with a sensitive child. And **[Ragnarök](/events/ragnarok)**, the myth of the world's eventual ending and rebirth, is the most intense of all — powerful and important to the mythology as a whole, but better saved for when a child is a little older and ready for a story about endings as well as beginnings. There's no need to rush toward these — the lighter stories hold up perfectly well on their own for years of bedtime reading.

## FAQ

**What is the best Norse myth to start with for a young child?**
Thor's adventures are usually the best entry point — he's brave, strong, a little goofy, and his stories tend to have clear, satisfying endings. The tale of the gods' treasures being made by dwarves, including Thor's hammer, is a favorite because it's funny and full of clever tricks. The [bedtime stories](/stories) collection is built for exactly this kind of first read.

**Is Norse mythology too scary for kids?**
Some of it is intense — particularly stories involving death, like Baldr's, and the world-ending myth of Ragnarök — but a large portion of Norse mythology is adventurous and funny rather than frightening. Choosing stories thoughtfully by age, the way you would with any collection of traditional tales, works well here.

**Who is the most kid-friendly Norse god?**
Thor tends to win kids over fastest: he's larger than life, endlessly enthusiastic, and always ready to swing his hammer at a monster. Freyja and Odin are also popular once kids get to know them, since Freyja is powerful and independent and Odin is mysterious in a way that invites questions.

**What is Yggdrasil, in simple terms?**
It's the giant tree that Norse mythology imagines holding up the whole universe, with different worlds arranged along its branches and roots. Thinking of it as the "trunk" that all nine realms are connected to is a simple, accurate way to picture it.

**Do I need to read the myths in order?**
Not at all. Norse myths work well as standalone stories — you can start anywhere that sounds fun, like Thor losing his hammer, and build up a bigger picture of the mythology over time as you read more.
