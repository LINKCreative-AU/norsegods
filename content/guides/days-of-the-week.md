Four days of the English week carry the names of Germanic gods in disguise: Tuesday belongs to Týr, Wednesday to Odin, Thursday to Thor, and Friday to Frigg (or possibly Freyja) — a fossil of pre-Christian religion still sitting in plain sight on every calendar. The pattern isn't a coincidence or folk etymology; it's the direct result of Germanic peoples translating a Roman naming system, god for god, sometime in the first few centuries CE, in a process scholars call *interpretatio germanica* — "Germanic interpretation" of Roman gods.

## The Roman system the Germanic peoples borrowed

The seven-day week itself is not native to northern Europe — it spread from the Roman world, where each day was dedicated to one of the seven visible "planets" of ancient astronomy (Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn), each of which was identified with a Roman god: *dies Solis* (Sun's day), *dies Lunae* (Moon's day), *dies Martis* (Mars's day), *dies Mercurii* (Mercury's day), *dies Iovis* (Jove/Jupiter's day), *dies Veneris* (Venus's day), and *dies Saturni* (Saturn's day). Some of these survive almost unchanged in the Romance languages — French *mardi*, *mercredi*, *jeudi*, *vendredi* still mean, literally, Mars's day, Mercury's day, Jove's day, and Venus's day.

When Germanic-speaking peoples adopted the seven-day week, probably through contact with the Roman world in the early centuries CE, they didn't just borrow the Latin names — they translated them, matching each Roman god to the Germanic god judged closest to him in character and function. This is exactly the same instinct behind pairing Norse and Greek gods for comparison today: ancient peoples in contact with each other's religions routinely mapped foreign gods onto their own pantheon's nearest equivalent.

## Tuesday: Týr's day

Tuesday comes from Old English *Tiwesdæg*, "Tiw's day" — Tiw being the Old English form of [**Týr**](/gods/tyr), the god associated with law, justice, and courageous, honorable warfare. Týr was matched to **Mars**, the Roman god of war, which makes sense on the surface (both are war gods), though the match is imperfect: Mars is a god of martial aggression, while Týr in the surviving Norse sources is defined above all by oath-keeping and sacrifice — most famously losing his hand in the mouth of the wolf Fenrir so the other gods could bind the monster safely, an act of courage in service of a promise rather than a battle. The equation of Týr with Mars still shows he was understood, at minimum, as the Germanic world's premier war-and-law god, the closest available match to Rome's.

## Wednesday: Odin's day

Wednesday comes from Old English *Wodnesdæg*, "Woden's day" — Woden being the Old English and continental Germanic name for [**Odin**](/gods/odin). Odin was matched to **Mercury**, which surprises people who expect the chief god to be paired with Jupiter, the king of the Roman gods. The logic becomes clear once you look at what Mercury and Odin actually do: Mercury is a wandering psychopomp who guides souls to the underworld, a god of eloquence, cunning, and secret knowledge, often shown with a staff and a wide hat — and Odin, too, wanders the world in disguise (frequently as a hooded, one-eyed traveler), gathers the souls of the slain, prizes cunning and poetry above brute strength, and traffics constantly in hidden knowledge. The Roman historian Tacitus, writing about Germanic religion in the first century CE, explicitly says the Germans worship Mercury above all other gods — almost certainly meaning Odin/Woden, described in Roman terms because Tacitus had no other vocabulary for a foreign god. It's a genuinely sharp piece of comparative theology, even though it long predates the modern academic study of religion.

## Thursday: Thor's day

Thursday comes from Old English *Þunresdæg*, "Thunor's day" — Thunor being the Old English and continental form of [**Thor**](/gods/thor), god of thunder. Thor was matched to **Jupiter** (also called Jove), the Roman sky-father and wielder of the thunderbolt — the most transparent and least controversial equation in the whole system, since both gods are, quite simply, gods of thunder who fight with a weapon thrown or wielded from the sky. This pairing is so intuitive that it survives almost unchanged across the whole Germanic-speaking world: German *Donnerstag*, Dutch *donderdag*, and English Thursday all preserve "Thunder's day" in some form.

## Friday: Frigg's day — or Freyja's?

Friday comes from Old English *Frigedæg*, matched to **Venus**, the Roman goddess of love — but which Norse goddess stands behind "Frige" is a genuine, unresolved scholarly debate rather than settled fact. The traditional answer is [**Frigg**](/gods/frigg), Odin's wife, matched to Venus presumably because both are married goddesses of high status connected to the domestic and marital sphere. But a strong scholarly case has also been made for [**Freyja**](/gods/freyja) instead: Freyja is far more directly a goddess of love and desire in the surviving Norse sources, and in later Scandinavian folk etymology "Friday" is explicitly Freyja's day (compare Old Norse *frjádagr*), while Frigg and Freyja themselves may originally have been a single goddess-figure who split into two over time — a theory some scholars support and others dispute. Complicating things further, Frigg belongs to the Æsir and Freyja to the Vanir, the two families of gods who were once rivals before their war ended in a lasting peace (see [Æsir vs. Vanir](/guides/aesir-vs-vanir)) — so the debate over Friday isn't just about which goddess's personality fits Venus better, but about which of two originally separate divine families left its mark on the English week. The honest answer is that the Old English evidence alone can't fully settle which goddess (or which fused version of both) the Anglo-Saxons had in mind when they translated Venus's day.

## Which days are NOT Norse

It's worth being precise here, because the pattern breaks on the remaining three days. **Saturday** comes straight from Latin *Saturni dies*, Saturn's day — the Germanic peoples apparently found no native god they considered an adequate match for Saturn, so this is the one day of the Roman week that was borrowed rather than translated. **Sunday** and **Monday** are Sun's day and Moon's day respectively, and while the Sun and Moon do appear as personified figures in Norse myth (Sól driving the sun's chariot, Máni driving the moon's, both pursued by wolves), these are astronomical bodies given divine personalities in both Roman and Germanic religion independently — they aren't examples of a Norse/Germanic god being substituted for a Roman one the way Tuesday through Friday are. So four days out of seven carry real, deliberate *interpretatio germanica* — Týr, Odin, Thor, and Frigg or Freyja — while the other three are either untranslated Roman borrowings or shared sky-imagery rather than direct god-for-god swaps.

## FAQ

**Why is Wednesday named after Odin instead of Thor, if Thor was more popular?**
Wednesday follows the Roman day of Mercury, and Mercury and Odin were judged the closest match because both are wandering, cunning gods linked to secret knowledge, eloquence, and guiding the dead — not because of relative popularity. Thor, the sky-thunder god, was matched instead to Jupiter, giving Thursday.

**Is Friday named for Frigg or Freyja?**
Both are seriously proposed by scholars, and the question isn't fully resolved. Frigg is the traditional answer as Odin's wife and a goddess of high status, but Freyja is more directly a love goddess matching Venus, and some scholars think Frigg and Freyja may descend from one original goddess who later split in two.

**Why isn't Saturday named after a Norse god?**
The Germanic peoples apparently had no god they considered an equivalent to Saturn, so instead of translating the Roman name they simply kept it — Saturday comes directly from Latin *Saturni dies*, Saturn's day, unlike Tuesday through Friday which were translated.

**What does interpretatio germanica mean?**
It's the scholarly term for the process by which Germanic peoples matched their own gods to Roman gods with similar roles or characters, translating the Roman week's day names into their own pantheon rather than borrowing the Latin names wholesale. Týr, Odin, Thor, and Frigg (or Freyja) are the results of that process.

**Did the Vikings themselves use these day names?**
Old Norse did have its own related day names — Old Norse *týsdagr*, *óðinsdagr*, *þórsdagr*, and *frjádagr* closely echo the English pattern — but the specific English forms (Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday) descend from Old English, reflecting a shared Germanic naming tradition rather than a direct Viking Age import into English.
